# Directing references: 30 moments to learn from (technique only, never look)

How to read this file: every entry names the TECHNIQUE in words, the exact
second it happens, why it works, and how we do it in our own look (lever,
ticket, sim clock, flat canvas, Unbounded/Inter/Space Mono). Timestamps come
from three honest sources, marked on each entry:
- [chapter] = the video's own chapter list in its description, read 3 Nov 2026.
- [transcript] = the tutorial's own transcript timestamps.
- [process] = the maker's own process description, whole video, no single second.
Nothing here is a guessed second. Where the file says "we would do", that is
our direction choice, not a claim about what the original video did.

Rule: we steal timing, structure and sound logic. We never steal a look, a
character, a colour system, a piece of music or a catchphrase.

## Camera: where the eye goes

### 1. A timestamped ordinary moment opens the run [chapter]
Video: What If – "What if the SUN went out for a day?"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-pC4cTS2JJU – 0:00 "The Sun switches off"
Technique: the premise lands inside a concrete clock line ("12:07 on a
Tuesday") instead of an abstract statement.
Why it works: a real clock makes the impossible feel scheduled, so the viewer
leans in to see the schedule break.
Our version: every long opens with a sim-clock line plus a place ("Tuesday,
12:07, your kitchen"), then the lever.

### 2. Push-in to the detail that does NOT change [chapter]
Same video – 1:06 "Minute one: what doesn't change"
Technique: after the premise, the camera goes to the first thing that stays
the same, not the first thing that breaks.
Why it works: negative space first makes the change land harder when it comes.
Our version: after the lever, one beat of "nothing changes yet" before the
first consequence. The sim clock ticks, the world holds.

### 3. The audience knows before the world does [chapter]
Video: What If – "What If the Sun Vanished Right Now"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mSxT998Xvx4 – 0:20 "The 8-minute ghost Sun"
Technique: the Sun is already gone, but the sky stays bright for 8 minutes 20
seconds, so the viewer watches people live inside a delay they understand.
Why it works: dramatic irony is a camera: it points attention at the gap
between knowledge and experience.
Our version: state the delay rule on screen ("light still travelling"), then
let the sim clock run through the gap before any consequence.

### 4. Scale jump by structure, not by image [chapter]
Video: What If – "What If You DIED on Every Planet in Our Solar System?"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AIlOpMfl6Wg – 04:07 Mercury
Technique: same setup every time ("you land, you die"), new kill mechanism
every time. The cut is a match cut of STRUCTURE.
Why it works: the repeated frame makes each new death comparable, and the
comparison is the joke and the lesson.
Our version: in escalation beats, keep the frame (same layout, same clock
position) and change only the subject and the number.

### 5. Rules in 12 seconds, then the run [chapter]
Video: "What If You Could Never Die?"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4MPZeWWXhnw – 0:12 "The rules of forever"
Technique: a whole chapter, 12 seconds long, that only states the rules of
the simulation before any story.
Why it works: the viewer grants the premise faster when the rules are short
and early; everything after feels fair.
Our version: the lever beat carries the rules in one line ("one rule: nobody
ages, everybody else does"), then the clock starts.

### 6. Body-scale cold open before any science [chapter]
Video: "What If Everyone Could Understand ANIMALS for a Week?"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pmtlul2zr5g – 0:40 "Hour 1: the 2 am walk"
Technique: the entire premise arrives as one household scene (a dog wanting a
walk at 2 am) before a single fact.
Why it works: the body understands a 2 am walk instantly; the science can
arrive after the viewer already cares.
Our version: the first escalation beat is always a household scene (phone,
kitchen, street), never a globe shot.

## Text: numbers and words as objects

### 7. A counter as a title [chapter]
Never Die – 0:00 "Three deaths, one answer"
Technique: the video opens by counting three deaths, on screen, before asking
the question.
Why it works: a counted list is a promise of structure; the viewer stays to
see the count complete.
Our version: the ticket prints "RUN #0xx" and the sim clock counts up; both
are counters the viewer can watch complete.

### 8. A statistic as an emotional object [chapter]
Never Die – 1:30 "Everyone you love is on a deadline"
Technique: life expectancy (about 73 years, WHO) is not shown as a chart; it
is shown as a deadline other people carry and you do not.
Why it works: a number becomes a feeling when it is attached to a person and
a clock.
Our version: big numbers always arrive with a familiar object or a person
next to them (see _REFERENCES.md, scale ladder). Never a bare number.

### 9. Time labels as chapter titles [chapter]
Sun Out – 2:08 "Hour one: the animals notice first"
Technique: every chapter is a clock reading ("Hour one", "Hour three", "Hour
twelve"), so the timeline IS the table of contents.
Why it works: the viewer can feel progress without a progress bar, and can
remember where they are.
Our version: this is our sim clock, made literal. The clock corner carries
the same job these chapter titles do.

### 10. Split frame: surface vs deep [chapter]
Sun Vanished – 4:20 "Frozen oceans and hidden life"
Technique: one frame holds two worlds at once (frozen surface above, hidden
life below) instead of cutting between them.
Why it works: comparison in one frame is faster than comparison across a cut,
and the contrast IS the point.
Our version: split screen world-now vs world-if, one of the BRAND wow moves.
Use it once per episode, at the twist's doorstep, not everywhere.

### 11. The recap card before the twist [chapter]
Animals – 5:43 "Three things we'd learn"
Technique: three short complete sentences, each on its own, right before the
twist chapter.
Why it works: a recap lowers cognitive load, so the twist has room to land;
without it the twist feels like a fourth fact, not a turn.
Our version: at about 60% of the run, three one-line cards (not a paragraph),
then silence, then the twist.

### 12. Name the project and the rule before showing anything [transcript]
School of Motion, "Kinetic Typography in After Effects, Part 3"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kYqXPW8Az2U – 00:22 in the transcript
Technique: the teacher opens by naming the project and the day ("day 18") and
the rule for the session before any demo.
Why it works: orientation before information; the viewer knows what kind of
attention to pay.
Our version: the lever + question + one rule line, always in that order, in
the first 3 seconds.

### 13. Offset colour for depth, not decoration [transcript]
Same tutorial – 25:15 in the transcript ("I put this blue color on and I just
sort of offset it a little bit just to give it a little bit of depth")
Technique: one flat colour, shifted a few pixels off its shape, reads as depth
without any 3D.
Why it works: the eye reads the offset as a shadow it does not have to
process, so the frame feels deep and stays cheap.
Our version: rim light and offset colour on flat shapes (already in BRAND
section 4). One offset, never a drop-shadow stack.

### 14. Arrive dead still, then move [transcript]
Same tutorial – 36:08 in the transcript (elements have "inherent motion on
the first frame"; the fix is to turn velocity to zero)
Technique: kill inherited motion on the first frame so every move starts from
a deliberate still.
Why it works: a still first frame makes the move that follows feel chosen,
and it kills the mushy "everything drifts" look.
Our version: every beat's main visual starts held for a beat, then moves.
Nothing drifts in from nowhere.

## Transitions: cuts that mean something

### 15. Hard cut to black as a transition [chapter]
Sun Vanished – 1:20 "Lights out"
Technique: the chapter itself is a cut to black; darkness is the wipe.
Why it works: the transition IS the event, so there is no decorative move to
explain away.
Our version: when a world state flips (lights, power, signal), cut to black
for a beat instead of crossfading. Black is our cheapest transition.

### 16. Escalating time jumps as transitions [chapter]
Sun Vanished – 2:30 "The cold arrives", then 3:10 "One week later", 4:00 "One
year later", 6:00 "Earth becomes a rogue planet"
Technique: the cuts are not between places but between TIMES, each jump bigger
than the last.
Why it works: acceleration is felt, not told; the edit rhythm teaches the
scale of the disaster.
Our version: the sim clock jumps (DAY 1, DAY 30, DAY 365) with a tick sound
each time; the jump size grows toward the twist.

### 17. Loop-back ending [chapter]
Sun Out – 8:17 "Hour twenty-four: the Sun comes back"
Technique: after 8 minutes of darkness, the video returns to its opening image
(the Sun, back on) before the coda.
Why it works: a return closes the loop the viewer has been holding open, and
the relief makes the coda's warning stick.
Our version: the vertical cut ends on a line that loops to its first line;
the long ends on the ticket, then the next episode's question (BRAND end
frame). Same job, different shape.

### 18. A second premise after the answer [chapter]
Sun Out – 9:12 "But if it never came back"
Technique: the answer is given (9:54 "The answer"), but first a whole second
premise runs: what if the dark never ends.
Why it works: the viewer came for one question and leaves with a bigger one;
the video feels larger than its runtime.
Our version: the twist chapter is our second premise. Never end on the
answer; end on the ticket plus the next question.

### 19. The twist gets its own chapter, labelled [chapter]
Sun Vanished – 7:30 "The twist"
Technique: the twist is not hidden; it is labelled as a chapter, at about 85%
of the runtime.
Why it works: labelling the twist makes viewers lean forward instead of
checking out; they know a turn is coming and stay for it.
Our version: twist at about 70% (BRAND), with the 0.4-0.8 s silence before it
and the Twist Coral colour reserved for it alone.

### 20. A promise before the cut [chapter]
Planets – 10:06 Mars ("And just wait till you see what happens on Mars. It's
unsettling." – description line 2-3)
Technique: the narrator promises the next beat before the cut to it.
Why it works: a promise is a retention device that costs one sentence and
feels like confidence, not clickbait.
Our version: end each escalation beat with one promised line ("wait till you
see the bill"), never with a summary.

### 21. The twist labelled honest [chapter]
Animals – 6:47 "The honest twist"
Technique: the twist chapter is called honest, and the description admits the
everyday scenes are imagined while the research is real.
Why it works: honesty is a retention device too; viewers forgive a guess they
were warned about and punish one they were not.
Our version: "Here's where the machine has to guess." on screen, every time,
plus the sources list in the description (BRAND section 7).

### 22. Serialized end frame [chapter]
Never Die – 4:20 "Part one ends"
Technique: the video ends mid-story with a part marker, not a conclusion.
Why it works: an open loop is the cheapest subscribe device that is not a
trick, because the story genuinely continues.
Our version: the end frame carries the NEXT episode's question (BRAND
section 6). Same open loop, inside one episode's runtime.

## Sound: the mix as direction

### 23. Voice locked before animation [process]
Video: Kurzgesagt – "How to Make a Kurzgesagt Video in 1200 Hours"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uFk0mgljtns (whole video; process also
described in the studio's own process notes: research, about a dozen script
drafts, about 200 illustrations, narration recorded per paragraph, then
animation timed to the voice, then a custom score)
Technique: narration is recorded BEFORE animation, and animation is timed to
it; music is composed LAST, for the finished cut.
Why it works: voice-first timing makes every move feel motivated; music-last
means the score serves the argument instead of papering over it.
Our version: same order, enforced by the pipeline: locked voice, then
animation, then music ducked under voice, dropout before the twist.

### 24. Tension music under calm pictures [process]
Video: RealLifeLore – "The Ocean is Way Deeper Than You Think"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GE-lAftuQgc (whole video; description credits
Ross Bugden's "Something Wicked", described as epic horror synth trailer
music, under a calm map video)
Technique: the music's emotion contradicts the picture's calm, and the
contradiction creates scale.
Why it works: contrast is information; the viewer feels depth before any
number is spoken.
Our version: wonder/momentum beds under calm flat scenes, tension bed only
when the sim clock accelerates. Never horror drones (BRAND section 5).

### 25. The mix empties when the world empties [chapter beat]
Sun Vanished – 6:50 "The air freezes"
Technique (our direction choice at this beat, not a claim about the original
mix): when the air itself freezes, drop the music and the ambience and leave
one sound, or none.
Why it works: silence after density reads as scale; the ear notices absence
faster than any new sound.
Our version: the BRAND silence rule, applied at the twist and at any beat
where the world loses something (air, light, signal).

### 26. One quiet line after escalation [chapter]
Never Die – 3:26 "When do I get forever with you?"
Technique: after chapters of cosmic escalation, one quiet personal question.
Why it works: loud-then-quiet is a dynamic range trick; the quiet line lands
because everything before it was loud.
Our version: after the biggest escalation beat, one short personal line
("who do you outlive?") before the twist. Never two loud beats in a row.

### 27. Recap as a sound bridge [chapter]
Animals – 0:00 "Last time, and tonight's what-if" (recap runs 0:00-0:40)
Technique: the first 40 seconds recap the previous episode over the new
episode's opening, so the recap IS the cold open.
Why it works: returning viewers get oriented and new viewers get a free
sample; nobody waits through a separate intro.
Our version: vertical cuts open with their own hook, never with a recap; the
long opens with the lever, never with a "last time". (We learn the device
and deliberately do NOT use it for the long form.)

## Scale and data: numbers you can feel

### 28. The household comparison, first line [chapter]
Planets – 00:00 Venus (description line 0: "If you landed on Venus, this hot
planet would crush you – like a plastic bottle.")
Technique: the first fact of the video arrives as a household object, not a
number (pressure in bars comes later, if at all).
Why it works: a plastic bottle is a scale the hand already knows; the number
would have been abstract.
Our version: the scale ladder rule (one familiar object per big number).
Never open a beat with a bare number.

### 29. A number as a place [chapter]
Sun Out – 3:19 "Hour three: how cold does it get?" (sources list Vostok
Station, -89.2 °C, 1983, World Meteorological Organization)
Technique: the coldest number in the video is attached to a named place
(Vostok) with a date, not floated as a statistic.
Why it works: a place with a date is a scene; a statistic is a slide.
Our version: every extreme number gets a place or a person in the same
sentence. "Minus 89 degrees" never appears without "Vostok, 1983".

### 30. The coda on the smallest thing [chapter]
Planets – 22:52 Pluto
Technique: after the biggest planets, the video ends on the smallest,
demoted one.
Why it works: a ladder needs a last rung, and the smallest rung is the joke
that proves the ladder was complete.
Our version: after the biggest escalation beat, one small quiet beat before
the ticket (a pet, a kitchen, a street). The coda is small on purpose.

## What we must NOT copy (looks, not techniques)

- Kurzgesagt's look: flat vector characters, bird mascots, their colour
  system, their music. We take voice-first timing and metaphor-first
  storyboarding, not the drawings.
- What If's 3D CGI look and its narrator persona. We take the chapter-as-clock
  structure, not the render style.
- Vox's yellow highlighter as a brand mark, and its newsprint collage look.
  We take the discipline (motion subordinate to information; one visual
  anchor per video; words timed to voice), not the yellow.
- RealLifeLore's map voice and its music choices. We take contrast (calm
  picture, tense bed), not the track.
- School of Motion's black-and-white kinetic type piece itself (MK12's
  Brazil lineage). We take the craft rules (orientation first, offset colour,
  arrive still), not the artwork.

  https://www.stashmedia.tv/buck-launches-ai-playground-for-nothing/

- Motionographer and School of Motion as ongoing study sources; award reels
  are for timing and transition vocabulary, never for look.
