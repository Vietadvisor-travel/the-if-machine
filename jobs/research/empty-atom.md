# Research pack — empty-atom · "What is inside an atom?" · FATHOM · Claude, 15 Nov 2026

## The question
An atom is far too small to see with any microscope. A century ago nobody knew what one looked like inside. How did a few people in a dark
room in Manchester find out — and why did what they found mean that everything, including you, is almost entirely empty?

## Facts
| # | fact | sources |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | 1897: J. J. Thomson discovered the electron (cathode rays) — atoms have parts | S1, S2 |
| F2 | 1904: Thomson's "plum pudding" model: electrons scattered through a ball of positive charge | S1, S2 |
| F3 | 1909, Manchester, Rutherford's lab: Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired alpha particles at very thin metal foil (gold, platinum) | S3, S4 |
| F4 | They counted particles one by one as tiny flashes (scintillations) on a zinc-sulphide screen, through a microscope, in the dark, with dark-adapted eyes | S4, S5, S6 |
| F5 | About 1 in 8,000 alpha particles bounced back (platinum, 1909 paper) — impossible in a plum pudding | S4, S7 |
| F6 | Rutherford later: "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you" (1936 lecture; wording possibly polished) | S8, S9 |
| F7 | May 1911, Philosophical Magazine: Rutherford — nearly all the mass and all the positive charge sit in a tiny centre: the nucleus | S3, S10 |
| F8 | The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller across than the atom: if the atom were a stadium, the nucleus would be a pea | S11, S12 |
| F9 | 1932: James Chadwick found the neutron, completing the nucleus | S1, S2 |
| F11 | Rutherford, a farmer's son from Nelson, New Zealand, won the 1908 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on radioactivity | S14, S15 |
| F12 | Alpha particles are helium nuclei, about 7,300 times the mass of an electron, emitted by radium at around 15,000 km/s (≈ 5% of light speed; kinetic energy ≈ 4.8 MeV); Geiger later built the self-counting Geiger counter (1908, improved 1928 with Müller) | S16, S17 |
| F13 | Ernest Marsden was born in 1889 (Rishton, Lancashire): twenty years old in 1909, an undergraduate at Manchester when Geiger trained him | S18, S19 |
| F14 | John Dalton's atomic theory (A New System of Chemical Philosophy, 1808): elements made of atoms with relative weights that combine in fixed ratios, pictured as solid spheres with his circle symbols | S1, S2 |
| F15 | A human hair is about 0.05–0.1 mm across; an atom about 0.1–0.3 nm — so hundreds of thousands of atoms side by side span one hair (no exact figure given) | S12, S13 |
| F16 | Geiger & Marsden 1913 (Phil. Mag. 25, pp. 604–623): counts at angles from 5° to 150° follow 1/sin⁴(φ/2), Rutherford's single-scattering law (Table II, X-01) | S6, S10 |
| F17 | Hydrogen, the simplest atom: one proton as its nucleus, one electron | S11, S13 |
| F18 | Chemical reactions involve only the outer electrons; the nucleus is unchanged (nuclear change is a different, far more energetic process) | S11, S13 |
| F10 | Everything solid is almost all empty space; it feels solid because electrons repel each other — your hand never truly "touches" the table | S11, S13 |

## Clues
1. **A part of an atom** — Thomson's electron and the pudding (F1, F2). 2. **The flashes in the dark** — Geiger & Marsden counting (F3, F4).
3. **The one that came back** — 1 in 8,000 (F5, F6). 4. **The pea in the stadium** — the nucleus (F7, F8, F9).
Twist: the solid world is nearly empty; what we feel as touch is a push between electrons (F10).

## Notes
- F5 is platinum in the 1909 paper; for gold Rutherford later quoted ~1 in 20,000 — say "about one in eight thousand bounced back" without naming the metal, or name platinum.
- F10 percentages ("99.9999…% empty") vary by definition — do not give a number; use the stadium/pea image.
- 16 Nov (production): Rutherford's exact 1936 words (T-01, p. 68): "It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." The script quotes "almost as incredible as if…".
- 16 Nov: the 1909 arrangement (D-01-x1 fig. 1) is drawn true — the screen beside the source behind a lead plate, a reflector of foil; the straight-through scenes use Geiger's evacuated tube; the angle-by-angle counts use the 1913 numbers (X-01, Table II, gold).
