# How the winners tell true stories — what we copy (Claude, 13 Nov 2026; owner: "content 5/10, learn from similar channels")

Limits: this session cannot open YouTube, Facebook or transcript archives (network allowlist), so the study uses Muse's scan of 60
Facebook videos with their hook lines (`fb_scan.csv`), published breakdowns of Zack D. Films and MrBallen, and short-form
script guides. Directional, not data from our own retention charts.

## What the winners do
1. **Zack D. Films** (16M FB followers, median 95K likes in our scan): 3D animation (2 of his 12 scanned videos are AI video), synthetic
   voice, no on-screen text, ~35–60 s. Every opening is a **concrete physical action at the start of the story**, subject omitted:
   "Pulled the spring from his flashlight and bent it into a hook." · "Used paper towels to seal himself into his hidden fort…" ·
   "Cast his fishing line at the woman until the hook caught on her jeans." · "Placed his sons in the lifeboat as the Titanic was
   sinking but instead of joining them, he kissed them goodbye…". Every sentence opens a small question the next one answers; a cut
   every 2–4 s; extreme close-ups of hands and objects; cross-sections to show the invisible; the ending loops to the opening.
   (Sources: fb_scan.csv; github.com/Anil-matcha/zack-d-films-ai-video-generator SKILL.md; outlierkit.com/channel/zackdfilms.)
2. **MrBallen** (true stories, campfire style): tension by **withholding** the key fact and letting the audience feel the narrator knows
   where it goes; strict chronology; real names, times, places; victims made into people (what they cared about), no gore; the reveal
   near the end. (Sources: Storytelling Lab episode on MrBallen's tension; Chris Williamson podcast summaries.)
3. **Ray William Johnson** (stock + voice, 56K): fast, spoken, specific: "Pilot in the US Navy and he's flying over Laos during the
   Vietnam War and suddenly kaboom his plane gets hit…" — a person, a place, a sudden event in the first breath.
4. Script guides agree: link beats with **"but" / "so"**, not "and then"; a re-hook every ~15–20 s in a 90–120 s piece; one new visual
   every 3–5 s; short sentences; cut the extra ending.

## Where our v2 (cook-under-the-sea, 1:45) falls short — owner score 5/10
- **Reported, not lived.** "He was trapped… breathing from one bubble of air" summarises; the winners put us INSIDE a moment
  ("The water was up to his chest. He couldn't see his own hand.").
- **No person.** Okene had a wife who had texted him psalms the night before, who did not know he was missing, whom he spent the
  dark hours worrying about (The Nation; Christian Post). That is the heart of the story and v2 never said it.
- **No withheld fact / no foreshadowing.** Everything is told in order with equal weight; nothing makes us lean in ("He did not know
  it yet, but the men coming for him were not looking for survivors.").
- **Flat "and then" chain.** Few "but" turns; numbers arrive as facts, not as threats (the air running out should be a clock).
- **Ending was a fact, not an irony.** The strongest true irony was missing: in December 2013 he said he would never go to sea again
  (AP/Deseret); two years later he went back under it — as a diver (Explorersweb; LADbible).

## The rules we adopt (folded into studio/STORY.md §3b)
1. Open on the story's own first concrete action or its single most unbelievable fact, said plainly — never a flash-forward.
2. Inside, not above: present-feeling detail (what he saw, heard, felt) from the sources, at least every 10 s.
3. One human thread from beginning to end (a person he loves, a promise, a habit) — only if sourced.
4. Withhold one thing the viewer wants to know and pay it off late; foreshadow once ("He did not know…").
5. Link beats with BUT / SO. A re-hook every 15–20 s: a new problem, a clock, a reveal.
6. Numbers as threats or clocks (air for ~60 h; he had been down ~60 h), not as trivia.
7. End on the true irony or meaning in one line, echoing the opening; no recap.
