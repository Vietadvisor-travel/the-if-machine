## Question (one line, as the viewer would ask it)

What if gravity was only half as strong, everywhere, forever?

## The answer (what the evidence actually says, one or two lines; objective, no storyline)

Half gravity feels like a superpower for a week, then your body starts dissolving like an astronaut's, because gravity is the workout you never notice.

## Entertaining angles (the facts that make people say "really?": surprising numbers, comparisons, history, records)
- Scott Kelly's heart lost 27% of its mass in 340 days, despite exercising almost every day in space.
- A six-month ISS mission costs an astronaut about as much bone as a postmenopausal woman loses in a full year.
- Apollo astronauts had to invent new gaits: loping, skipping, kangaroo hopping, because normal walking does not work at one sixth gravity.
- You would grow about 2 inches taller, and your heart would shrink at the same time.

## Notes / traps (numbers that are easy to get wrong, claims to avoid)
- Half gravity is NOT zero gravity: keep the "half, not zero" framing clear, or viewers expect a zero-g video.
- Bone loss figures are for microgravity (near zero g), not half g: present them as the direction and the mechanism, not as the exact half-gravity rate. Nobody has lived at half g.
- F3: the 17-astronaut study found trabecular bone "did not fully recover in most participants" after a year; do not overstate as "9 of 17" without the paper in hand.
- Kelly's heart recovered after return; the 27% is the in-flight change, not permanent damage.

## Addendum (Claude, 10 Nov 2026, production): facts checked and added
- F4 corrected: the reliable figure is that Scott Kelly's left ventricle (the heart's main pumping chamber) lost about 0.74 g of mass per week over 340 days, despite exercise six days a week, with function still good (UT Southwestern newsroom, 30 Mar 2021, https://www.utsouthwestern.edu/newsroom/articles/year-2021/astronauts-heart-left-smaller.html · News-Medical, 30 Mar 2021, https://www.news-medical.net/news/20210330/Researchers-study-the-physical-effects-of-space-travel-on-astronauts-heart.aspx; study: MacNamara, Levine et al., Circulation 2021). The "27 %" and "189 → 138 g" are NOT used (outlets disagree).
- F2 reworded: NASA's long-standing benchmark is 1 to 1.5 % of bone mineral density per month on long missions (NASA NTRS 20170004598, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20170004598 · Space.com, https://www.space.com/6354-space-station-astronauts-lose-bone-strength-fast.html).
- F3 sharpened: Gabel et al. 2022 (17 ISS astronauts): a year after landing, shin (tibia) density was still on average 2.1 % lower, and 9 of 17 had not recovered (NBC/Inquirer summaries of Scientific Reports 2022, https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/?p=1620679 · https://www.doi.org/10.1038/S41526-020-0103-2 context).
- F11 (NEW, the half-g test): JAXA's MARS centrifuge on the ISS (Kibo) kept 24 mice at 0, 0.33, 0.67 and 1 g for about 28 days: 0.67 g preserved muscle and grip strength; 0.33 g softened the loss but did not fully protect muscle function. Half gravity sits between the two (Science Advances, 13 Mar 2026, doi 10.1126/sciadv.aed2258, PMC12985678 · ScienceAlert, https://www.sciencealert.com/?p=195821 · Space Connect, https://www.spaceconnectonline.com.au/discovery/7013-jaxa-nasa-partner-on-research-to-reveal-how-much-gravity-humans-really-need).
- F12 (NEW, walking): people switch from walking to running at about the same Froude number (v²/gL ≈ 0.5) in reduced gravity, so the switch comes at slower speeds: at half g, about 0.7 × the Earth speed (Kram, Domingo & Ferris, J Exp Biol 200:821, 1997, PubMed 9076966 · NASA NTRS 20040173033).
- F13 (NEW, air): surface air pressure is the weight of the air column; at half g it would be about half of today's, which is the pressure near 5,500 m altitude today (REI Expert Advice "Why We Struggle at Altitude", https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/why-we-struggle-at-altitude.html · Pfeiffer Vacuum know-how book, https://know-how-book.pfeiffer-vacuum.com/en/introduction-to-vacuum-technology/general/overview-of-vacuum/index.html). Water at half an atmosphere (≈ 50 kPa) boils at about 81 °C (steam tables; Engineering ToolBox, https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/boiling-points-water-altitude-d_1344.html). The half-g step is our reasoning, said as such.
- F14 (NEW, the twist): a planet on a circular orbit moves at exactly the escape speed for half the mass (v_circ = √(GM/r), v_esc = √(2GM/r)). So if the Sun's pull halved at once, Earth (≈ 30 km/s) would be right at the edge: unbound or on a huge, cold loop depending on where it is in its slightly oval orbit. Astronomy magazine (May 2005 Q&A, https://www.astronomy.com/science/after-our-sun-dies-will-the-planets-still-orbit-it/: "if the Sun lost half its mass instantly, the planets would fly off") · Astrobites, 16 Jan 2012, https://astrobites.org/2012/01/16/the-fate-of-the-solar-system-6-billion-years-along-the-road/ (slow loss keeps planets bound; sudden loss does not). The Moon around Earth is in the same position.
- F15 (NEW, rockets): orbital speed √(gR) drops from about 7.9 to about 5.6 km/s at half g (physics; NASA orbital-speed basics), so getting to orbit gets far cheaper. Said as a calculation.
- F6 corrected: the space-agency figure is "up to 20 % of muscle mass on flights of five to eleven days" (Canadian Space Agency, https://asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/osm/muscles.asp · ASME, https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/study-targets-effects-space-travel-muscles, citing NASA). The "six months" wording is NOT used.
