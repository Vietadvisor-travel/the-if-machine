## Key numbers (exact, with unit and source; "about" where the sources disagree)

- Start: 14 July 1518 (Public Domain Review; Britannica).
- Frau Troffea danced: 4-6 days (Public Domain Review).
- Distance to Saverne: 30 miles by wagon; the later shrine trip took three days (Public Domain Review).
- Seized within a month: 400 citizens, per the Imlin chronicle (Public Domain Review).
- Duration: more than a month, mid-July to late August or early September (Public Domain Review).
- Deaths: a number died; the toll is unknown and disputed. Do NOT state a toll. The "15 deaths a day at the peak" figure comes from later accounts, not the council minutes; the Public Domain Review repeats it only with "if such a daily death rate was true". Never use it as fact.

## Quotes (exact words only, with who said them and the source; no invented dialogue)

1. "Many hundreds in Strassburg began / To dance and hop, women and men, / In the public market, in alleys and streets, / Day and night; and many of them ate nothing / Until at last the sickness left them. / This affliction was called St Vitus' dance." Lost manuscript poem, quoted by the 17th-century Strasbourg jurist Johann Schilter, via Public Domain Review, 10 Jul 2018.
2. "In the year 1518 AD... there occurred among men a remarkable and terrible disease called St Vitus' dance, in which men in their madness began to dance day and night until finally they fell down unconscious and succumbed to death." Chronicle of 1636, via Public Domain Review, 10 Jul 2018.
3. "A natural disease, which comes from overheated blood." The Strasbourg physicians' diagnosis, via Public Domain Review, 10 Jul 2018.
4. "If honourable persons wish to dance at weddings or celebrations of first Mass in their houses, they may do so using stringed instruments, but they are on their conscience not to use tambourines and drums." Sebastian Brant, Strasbourg chancellor, on the exception to the dance ban, via Public Domain Review, 10 Jul 2018.
5. "The minds of the choreomaniacs were drawn inwards, tossed about on the violent seas of their deepest fears." Historian John Waller, A Time to Dance, a Time to Die (2008), via Public Domain Review, 10 Jul 2018.
6. "In order to make the deception as perfect as possible, and really give the impression of illness, she hopped and sang, which was all most distasteful to her husband." Paracelsus, Opus Paramirum, on Frau Troffea, via Public Domain Review, 10 Jul 2018. Use only as "one 16th-century theory", never as fact; Paracelsus also blamed the wives who copied her.

## Visual notes (place, era, weather/light, clothing, objects, vehicles, what the room/landscape looked like)

- Strasbourg, July 1518: narrow cobbled streets, half-timbered homes, the busy horse market and the grain market.
- The stage: a hastily built platform before the horse market; pipes, drums, fiddles and horns; July sun beating down; dancers hopping from leg to leg, spinning in circles, whooping loudly; arms flailing, bodies convulsing; ragged clothes and pinched faces saturated in sweat; glassy, distant eyes; blood seeping from swollen feet into leather boots and wooden clogs.
- The cure that backfired: carpenters and tanners turning guild halls into dance floors, healthy dancers brought in for encouragement.
- The ban: stages pulled down, the city quiet, no drums.
- The shrine at Saverne: priests placing dancers under a wooden carving of St Vitus, small crosses in their hands, red shoes on their feet, holy water sprinkled on the soles and tops, crosses of consecrated oil, incense and Latin incantations.

## Sensitivity (victims, families, living people, contested points) and how to tell it with respect

- Deaths are real but the numbers are contested; never state a death toll, never use the "15 a day" figure as fact.
- Do not mock the dancers. The tone is awe at a city losing its mind, not a joke about dancing.
- Paracelsus blamed the women; present his theory as a 16th-century view, never as the explanation. The modern explanation is mass psychogenic illness under famine and disease stress, and even that is an explanation, not a proven cause.
- The turn is the city's own cure making it worse, not the suffering.
