# Research pack — longitude · "How do you know where you are on the open sea?" · FATHOM · Claude, 15 Nov 2026

## The question
At sea, latitude is easy: the Sun and the Pole Star tell you how far north you are. But east–west, there is nothing to see — only water.
For centuries ships simply guessed, and many were lost. How did we learn to know our longitude?

## Facts
| # | fact | sources |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours → each hour of difference between local time and home time = 15° of longitude. Longitude is a clock problem | S1, S2 |
| F2 | Night of 22 Oct 1707: Admiral Shovell's fleet struck the Western Rocks of the Isles of Scilly; four warships lost, about 1,400–2,000 men; the fleet had misjudged its position | S3, S4, S5 |
| F3 | 1714: the Longitude Act offered up to £20,000 for a method accurate to half a degree (£10,000 for one degree) | S5, S6 |
| F4 | Two ways competed: the astronomers' "lunar distance" (the Moon as a clock in the sky, with tables) and a sea-going clock — thought impossible (pendulums fail on a rolling ship; heat, cold) | S2, S7 |
| F5 | John Harrison, a carpenter from Yorkshire, self-taught clockmaker (early clocks of wood); H1 took about five years, tested on a voyage to Lisbon in 1736 | S8, S9 |
| F6 | H4 — a large watch, not a clock — went to Jamaica on HMS Deptford in 1761–62 with his son William; commonly reported error about 5 seconds | S10, S11 |
| F7 | 1767: Nevil Maskelyne's Nautical Almanac made lunar distances practical (calculation from ~4 hours to ~1) | S12, S13 |
| F8 | The Board of Longitude never paid the full prize; in 1773, urged by King George III, Parliament awarded Harrison £8,750 | S14, S15 |
| F9 | Captain Cook carried K1, Larcum Kendall's copy of H4, on his second voyage (1772–75) and praised it | S12, S13 |
| F10 | H1–H4 are at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich. Today GPS finds position from time signals of atomic clocks on satellites | S9, S16 |

## Clues
1. **The missing clock** — longitude = time (F1). 2. **The rocks** — Scilly 1707 and the prize (F2, F3).
3. **Two rivals** — the Moon tables vs the carpenter's clocks (F4, F5, F7). 4. **The watch at sea** — H4 to Jamaica (F6, F8).
Twist: the answer to "where am I?" was "what time is it?" — and it still is: every phone finds itself from clocks in orbit (F10).
