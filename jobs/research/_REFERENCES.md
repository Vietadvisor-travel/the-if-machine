# Reference study: what the big channels do, and what The If Machine steals, avoids, and owns

Date: 3 Nov 2026. Written by Muse for the engine phase (jobs/tasks/MUSE_ENGINE_PHASE.md, task 3).
Purpose: give Claude concrete, watchable devices for the canvas engine. This is a study of
technique, not a mood board. Nothing here is permission to copy a look, a character, a piece
of music, or a title formula.

Honesty note on numbers: subscriber and view counts below are third party readings
(OutlierKit, vidIQ, Trari, TubeGrade, Inside Createurs) taken 2-3 Nov 2026, not YouTube's
own analytics. They are good enough to compare channels, not good enough to quote in a video.
"Cuts per minute" was NOT counted frame by frame for this study; where a source measured
something (voice start time, chapter structure), it is cited. Everything else about pace is a
qualitative observation from watching the format, labelled as such.

## 1. The channels

### What If (Underknown) - the direct ancestor, and the warning
- Scale (Nov 2026): 9.0M subscribers, 1.8B total views, about 1,700 videos, about 1.0M views
  per video lifetime, recent uploads averaging about 20:23. Long-form is 91% of views.
- Cadence: several videos a week across the network; catalogue 92% evergreen.
- Momentum warning: OutlierKit rates recent momentum 0.66x, slowing. A 20 minute, heavy 3D
  CGI video is expensive to make and slow to ship. That is the gap a 5-6 minute canvas
  channel can run through: same question shape, one third of the length, three times a week.
- Hook: the title IS the question, and the cold open states a body-scale consequence in
  the first sentence ("crush you like a plastic bottle... five seconds"). No preamble.
- Structure: one scenario, walked in order, with on-screen chapters. The planet tour video
  below is literally a checklist with a timer per stop.
- Sound: one calm narrator (Peter Schmiedchen), cinematic music kept low, sound design
  only at the moment of impact. The voice never competes with the music.
- Titles: "What If [extreme thing]?" plus a parenthetical twist ("(SIMULATION)").
  Recent breakout: "What If Every Asteroid Hit Earth at Once? (SIMULATION)", 2.8M views,
  about 5x the channel's recent median.
- Top 3 what-if videos: "What If You DIED on Every Planet in Our Solar System?"
  (16.95M, Dec 2024); "What Was Earth Like Before the Dinosaurs?" (7.76M, Apr 2023);
  "What If a 10km Asteroid Was Just the Start of Earth's 4.5 Billion Year War?" (7.6M,
  Jul 2023).
- Steal: consequence first, checklist structure, chapter clock. Avoid: the 3D CGI look,
  the 20 minute length, the disaster-only diet.

### Kurzgesagt - In a Nutshell - the quality ceiling, not the target
- Scale: 25.8M subscribers, 4.0B total views, 397 videos, about 10.1M views per video,
  recent long videos averaging about 12:20. A new long video usually gets about 11.6M views
  (Trari); a new short about 1.4M.
- Cadence: about 0.4 videos a week, roughly 1-2 a month, with 98% on-schedule adherence.
  A Munich studio of 30-50+ people reportedly spends 1,200-3,000 team hours per 10 minute
  video. Not replicable, and not the race to run. The lesson is the opposite: their
  slowness leaves the weekly what-if slot open.
- Hook, measured: in a 50 video study of big channels (27 Sep 2026), Kurzgesagt's voice
  starts at 0.00 seconds in both sampled videos. No title card, no cold open, no logo
  sting before the first word. The first sentence IS the hook.
- Motion: slow, confident camera drifts; one idea per shot; objects build in rather than
  cutting away. Colour is flat with soft gradients and rim light, which is close to our
  BRAND section 4 language but is THEIR palette, not ours. Our palette is Midnight,
  Lever Yellow, Twist Coral, Sim Teal.
- Sound: original composed music, warm narrator (Steve Taylor), silence used sparingly
  before a reveal. Music never shouts.
- Titles: plain questions or statements, no clickbait adjectives. Recent 30 day top:
  "AI Just Crossed the Terrifying Line - Now What?" (23.3M views). Trend pegging works
  even for a slow channel.
- Steal: voice at second zero, one idea per shot, confidence. Avoid: their birds, ducks,
  flat space palette, narrator identity, and any attempt to match their finish. Our finish
  is code-drawn flat colour, not theirs.

### Zack D. Films - the Shorts machine, and the loop lesson
- Scale: 29.4M subscribers, 81.4B total views, about 4,100 videos, about 19.7M views per
  video, 100% Shorts. Channel started June 2021.
- Format: one strange fact, one survival trick, or one "what would happen if" per video,
  30-60 seconds, cinematic 3D animation, calm narrator, no host.
- Hook, measured by an independent breakdown of the 253M view "How Stitches Work" short
  (31 seconds): the needle is at the skin in the FIRST SECOND. The video states the
  question immediately and ends the moment curiosity ends. A 31 second video watched
  1.4 times beats a 60 second video abandoned halfway. The loop is the retention trick.
- Packaging: the title does the thumbnail's job (a handful of words, exactly one emoji,
  every time). The description of that 253M view video is EMPTY. No keywords, no hashtags,
  no links. For long-form this does not transfer (our descriptions carry sources), but
  for vertical cuts the lesson holds: title and first frame carry everything.
- Sound: a signature background music bed, instantly recognisable across videos, plus
  the same calm narration cadence. Sound is brand, not decoration.
- Scale warning: public job listings pay about $1,000 flat per video at about 40 projects
  a week. That is a studio, not a solo creator. Do not try to match the 3D finish.
- Steal: first second contract, end at the payoff, loopable vertical ending, one question
  per video. Avoid: the 3D look, the medical/gross-out diet, the empty description habit
  on long-form.

### The Infographics Show - the volume engine, and the clock
- Scale: 15.5M subscribers, 7.0B total views, about 6,370 videos, about 1.1M views per
  video. Recent uploads average about 38:40, but that number is inflated by compilations;
  the core explainers run 8-15 minutes.
- Cadence: about 9 videos a week, near daily. A rolling programming grid in the
  North America / Europe early evening window. Volume is the strategy, and it needs a
  team; our answer is 3 long videos a week, not 9.
- Structure device that matters most for us: the MINUTE BY MINUTE clock. "How SEAL Team
  Took Down Osama bin Laden (Minute by Minute)" has 20.73M views; "What If North Korea
  Launched a Nuclear Bomb (Minute by Minute)" has 13.65M. A clock on screen turns a list
  of facts into a countdown the viewer cannot leave. Our sim clock (DAY 1 to DAY 365,
  HOUR 0 to HOUR 24) is the same device, generalised to every topic.
- Visuals: highly stylised 2D animation, simple character designs, bold colours, HEAVY
  on-screen text guiding the viewer at a rapid but digestible pace. Text does half the
  teaching. Our subtitle rule (Inter Bold, spoken word highlighted yellow, max 2 lines)
  is the same instinct with a stricter frame.
- Sound: fast narration, music bed that never stops, stings on every beat change.
  Faster than ours should be: our narrator pace is 150-160 words per minute, theirs is
  noticeably quicker, and it costs warmth.
- Steal: the clock as narrative spine, text that teaches, compilation recycling of
  evergreen episodes later. Avoid: the pace, the character style, the anxiety diet
  (war, survival, geopolitics), the daily volume.

### AsapSCIENCE - the personality moat, and the participation trick
- Scale: 10.8M subscribers, 2.1B total views, 561-568 videos, about 3.7M views per video,
  recent uploads averaging about 9:08. A new long video usually gets about 650K views.
- Cadence: roughly monthly now, with bursts. The channel is 14 years old; the moat is the
  two hosts, not the format.
- Format: colourful whiteboard drawings, stop motion feel, voiceover, jokes and puns,
  categories, and a final "so what does this mean for you?" payoff. The payoff structure
  is worth stealing: every video ends by pointing the science back at the viewer's life.
- Participation device: the 66M view "Do You Hear Yanny or Laurel?" video plays the
  sound, asks the viewer what THEY hear, then pitch shifts it so both answers appear.
  The viewer is an experiment subject, not an audience. Our version: ask the viewer to
  guess the answer before the twist, then print the ticket. Guessing is engagement.
- Sound: one host voices and composes the background music himself. Music is simple,
  light, and never competes with the voice.
- Steal: the participation moment, the "what does this mean for you" payoff, the joke
  every 30 seconds (already in our BRAND). Avoid: the whiteboard look, the host
  personalities (we are faceless by rule), the taboo-body diet.

### TED-Ed - the rotating style, and the short script
- Scale: 23M subscribers, 4.77B total views, about 2,300 videos, a typical recent video
  about 668K views. About 2 uploads a week.
- Format: 4-7 minute animated lessons (average about 6 minutes per AIR Media-Tech's
  2026 animation survey), each made by a different expert plus a different animator.
  Scripts run 800-900 words, simple and direct, and a video takes at least four months
  from script to release. Founder Logan Smalley reports about 100 videos a year at well
  over 1.5M views per video on average across the catalogue.
- The device that matters: EVERY video has a different art style, yet every video is
  instantly TED-Ed. The brand frame (title card, pacing, narration quality, lesson
  shape) holds, while the world inside changes. This validates our BRAND rule directly:
  in-story worlds keep real colours, brand colours are the frame. Our lever, ticket,
  sim clock, and fonts are the frame; the world inside can change every episode.
- Pacing: meticulous, visual cues reinforcing narration, key points repeated visually.
  Editing guides attention rather than showing off.
- Steal: the frame-not-world rule, the 800-900 word discipline (our 5-6 minute scripts
  land in the same band at 150-160 words per minute), the lesson shape. Avoid: the
  classroom tone, the rotating freelancers model, the 4 month pipeline.

### RealLifeLore - the scale ladder, and the slow first minute
- Scale: 7.9M subscribers, 2.0B total views, 496 videos, about 4.0M views per video.
  Recent uploads average about 42:36 (long documentaries now), but the best performing
  band is mid-form 3-20 minutes, median 3.1M views, which is 13x the short-form median.
- Format: custom map animations, data visualisations, confident narration, dramatic
  framing of geography, logistics, and demographic anomalies.
- The device that matters: the SCALE LADDER. "The Ocean is Way Deeper Than You Think"
  (50.3M views, the channel's top video) walks down from things the viewer knows
  (a swimmer, a submarine) to things nobody can picture (the average ocean depth,
  Challenger Deep), comparing each step to a familiar landmark. Numbers grow into
  shapes. Our BRAND already wants this ("numbers growing into shapes" in section 4);
  RealLifeLore is the proof it works at 50M views.
- Hook, measured: in the same 50 video study, RealLifeLore's voice starts at 0.40 and
  0.48 seconds, and the first minute runs SLOWER than the rest of the video (0.68x).
  A slow, confident first minute is allowed when the promise is big. Contrast with
  Kurzgesagt (voice at 0.00, same pace throughout) and Zack D (first second contract).
  Our rule: voice at second zero like Kurzgesagt, but the first 30 seconds set rules
  and promise like RealLifeLore's slow open, not a frantic montage.
- Top 3: "The Ocean is Way Deeper Than You Think" (50.3M); "What's the Deepest Hole We
  Can Possibly Dig?" (48.4M); "How the Universe is Way Bigger Than You Think" (33.1M).
  Note the pattern: all three are "X is way bigger/deeper than you think" scale videos.
- Steal: the scale ladder, the familiar-landmark comparison, the confident slow open.
  Avoid: the map style, the geopolitics diet, the 40 minute length.

### Curiosity Hub - the direct competitor, launched days ago
- Scale: brand new. Trailer posted days before this study. No subscriber or view history
  worth quoting yet, which is exactly why it matters: it is a new channel doing OUR
  exact product, animated what-ifs, and it will grow or die in public at the same time
  as us.
- Format from its trailer: "Every week we take a huge hypothetical scenario, about
  space, science, Earth, the human body, or artificial intelligence, and work out what
  would actually happen, step by step." New what-if videos every Sunday, new Shorts
  every Saturday, Monday, and Wednesday.
- First full episode: "What If You Could Stop Time for 24 Hours?" Announced next:
  "What If the Sun Disappeared Tomorrow?" and "What If AI Replaced Every Job by 2035".
- Collision risk, concrete: our bank topic "time freezes except you, for one year"
  overlaps their 24 hour version. Differentiation is mandatory: ours runs a full YEAR,
  uses the machine format (lever, sim clock, ticket), and ends with an answer ticket,
  not a story. If their episode lands first, our title and hook must not look like a
  copy. Same watch on "AI replaced every job" versus our "AI runs a country": different
  question, but do not let the two blur in titles or thumbnails.
- Steal: the weekly cadence discipline, the public roadmap (announcing next topics
  builds anticipation; our end frame already does this with the next episode's
  question). Avoid: everything else about their look until it is established, and never
  react to them by copying a topic. Our bank decides; their schedule does not.

### Rising small channels - proof the format works without a studio
- A wave of small 2026 what-if channels runs the exact shape we plan: chapter clocks
  (hour by hour, day by day), synthetic narration, and a sources list. One planet tour
  video states in its own description "Narration voice: synthetic (Kokoro TTS)" and
  lists NASA sources with a checked date. Another sun-out video runs chapters from
  "Minute one: what doesn't change" to "Hour twenty-four: the Sun comes back" and
  closes with "The answer". A third states plainly "The everyday scenes in this video
  are imagined. The animal research is real."
- Lesson: the clock plus honest labelling plus a sources list is already a working
  small-channel format. Our advantage over them is not the format, it is the machine
  brand (lever, ticket, rituals), the 3x a week cadence, and the research standard
  (every heavy claim sourced, guesses labelled on screen).

## 2. Fifteen wow moments to study (watch these, steal the device, never the look)

1. The crush in five seconds. What If, "What If You DIED on Every Planet in Our
   Solar System?", 0:00. "If you landed on Venus, this hot planet would crush you,
   like a plastic bottle. It would be painful. But don't worry, it would only last
   five seconds." A body-scale consequence, a household object comparison, and a time
   limit in the first two sentences. Device: consequence plus comparison plus clock.
   Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AIlOpMfl6Wg
2. The checklist clock. Same video, chapters 00:00 Venus, 04:07 Mercury, 10:06 Mars,
   14:16 Jupiter, 16:33 Saturn, 18:24 Uranus, 20:29 Neptune, 22:52 Pluto. The viewer
   always knows where they are in the tour. Device: visible progress, not chapters
   hidden in a description. Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AIlOpMfl6Wg
3. The polite ranking. Small channel planet tour, "What If You Died on Every Planet?
   (A Polite Tour)", 8:24 "The ranking, politely". After eight minutes of deaths, the
   video ranks them. A ranking is a reason to watch to the end. Device: promise a
   ranking early, deliver it last. Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M2MowNWv_2o
4. The sources list with a checked date. Same video's description: "Sources (checked
   2026-10-16)" followed by NASA fact sheets per planet. Trust is a feature, and a
   checked date is cheaper than a studio. Device: date-stamped sources, in every
   description. Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M2MowNWv_2o
5. The 79 second what-if. Kurzgesagt Short, "What If We Cool Down the Sun?", 1:19
   total, 949,977 views, 515,759 on day one. A whole hypothetical, question to answer,
   in 79 seconds. Proof our 60-90 second vertical is not a compromise; it is a format
   the biggest animation channel also ships. Device: one rule, one escalation, one
   answer, no fat. Link: https://www.thefuturist.co/what-if-we-cool-down-the-sun/
6. The scale ladder. RealLifeLore, "The Ocean is Way Deeper Than You Think". The
   descent passes a swimmer, then emperor penguins diving deeper (535 metres) than a
   nuclear submarine (443 metres), then permanent darkness past 1,000 metres, then the
   average ocean depth (4,267 metres), then Challenger Deep (10,994 metres), which is
   as far down as a commercial airplane flies high. Every step uses a familiar object.
   Device: never state a big number without a familiar object beside it.
   Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GE-lAftuQgc
7. The participation test. AsapSCIENCE, "Do You Hear Yanny or Laurel? (SOLVED with
   SCIENCE)", 66M views. Play the sound, ask the viewer what they hear, then pitch
   shift it until both answers appear. The viewer discovers the science in their own
   ears. Device: make the viewer run the experiment before you explain it.
   Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yDiXQl7grPQ
8. The first second contract. Zack D. Films, "How Stitches Work" (31 seconds, 253M
   views, analysed in the breakdown linked below). The needle is at the skin before
   the viewer decides whether to swipe. The video ends the moment curiosity ends and
   loops. Device: first frame already mid-action, last frame loops to the first.
   Analysis: https://danielrusnok.substack.com/p/how-a-faceless-channel-engineered
9. The hour by hour clock. Small channel, "What if the SUN went out for a day?",
   chapters 0:00 the Sun switches off, 1:06 minute one, 2:08 hour one (the animals
   notice first), 3:19 hour three (how cold), 5:31 hour eight (food and air), 8:17 hour
   twenty-four (the Sun comes back), 9:12 if it never came back, 9:54 the answer.
   The clock IS the story structure. Device: time labels as chapter titles.
   Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-pC4cTS2JJU
10. The labelled twist. Small channel, "What If the Sun Vanished Right Now", chapters
    0:00 vanish, 0:20 the 8-minute ghost Sun, 1:20 lights out, 2:30 the cold arrives,
    3:10 one week later, 4:00 one year later, 4:20 frozen oceans and hidden life,
    5:00 could humans survive, 6:00 Earth becomes a rogue planet, 6:50 the air freezes,
    7:30 THE TWIST, 7:50 where life really began. The twist has its own chapter, near
    the end, like our 70% twist. Device: name the twist on screen, do not hide it.
    Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mSxT998Xvx4
11. The emotional twist, not a physics twist. Small channel, "What If You Could Never
    Die?", 1:30 "Everyone you love is on a deadline you don't share". After a century
    of fun, the twist is grief, not a number. Device: the best twist in a human
    what-if is emotional, and it lands harder because the first half was fun.
    Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4MPZeWWXhnw
12. Imagined scenes, real research. Small channel, "What If Everyone Could Understand
    ANIMALS for a Week?", chapters hour 1 (the dog wants a 2am walk), hour 3 (possums
    fighting about a mango), hour 7 (the cat), day 2 (prairie dogs describe you), day 4
    (elephants have names), day 5 (the whale alphabet), 6:47 the honest twist. Tiny
    everyday scenes carry real animal research, and the description states "The
    everyday scenes in this video are imagined. The animal research is real."
    Device: separate the imagined from the researched, out loud. That sentence is our
    honesty model in one line. Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pmtlul2zr5g
13. The one-take timeline. Small channel, "What If You Kept Evolving and Couldn't
    Stop?", chapters 0:00 a single cell, 0:33 many cells and first eyes, 0:54 you
    become a fish, 5:09 machines, 5:27 1944, 5:52 the Moon, 6:09 disco, 6:24 today,
    6:53 tomorrow, 7:17 post-human, 7:40 Mars, 8:09 another sun, 9:07 the end. No
    narration, just captions and a clock, 3.8 billion years in one continuous zoom
    forward. "Disco" as a one second beat is the joke. Device: a single continuous
    camera move through time, with jokes as mile markers. The video states it was
    "made entirely with code: every creature, world and sound generated frame by
    frame", which is our production story too.
    Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NxZIM4YhKxg
14. The minute by minute spine, at 20M scale. The Infographics Show, "How SEAL Team
    Took Down Osama bin Laden (Minute by Minute)", 20.73M views, and "What If North
    Korea Launched a Nuclear Bomb (Minute by Minute)", 13.65M. The clock is not
    decoration; it is the reason to keep watching, because the next minute is a
    promise. Device: a clock that counts UP toward a known endpoint beats a clock
    that just decorates. Our sim clock should always count toward the ticket.
    Channel: https://outlierkit.com/channel/theinfographicsshow
15. The trailer as promise machine. Curiosity Hub trailer (60 seconds):
    "Every week we take a huge hypothetical scenario and work out what would actually
    happen, step by step. New what if videos every Sunday." Then it lists the next
    topics out loud. A trailer that names the next three questions is a subscription
    device, not a sizzle reel. Device: our end frame already names the next episode's
    question; treat it as seriously as the video itself.
    Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mmoiqld0dXk

## 3. What we learn (transferable devices, in priority order)

1. Voice at second zero, no logo sting first. Kurzgesagt measured at 0.00s. Our
   lever sound plus question must land inside the first 3 seconds WITH the voice,
   not before it.
2. The clock is the story. What If chapters, Infographics minute by minute, the small
   channels' hour by hour clocks, and our sim clock are one device. The clock must
   always count toward a known endpoint (the ticket), and time labels should be
   chapter titles, not decoration.
3. Consequence first, explanation second. Venus crushes you in five seconds, then the
   physics. The Sun switches off, then the 8 minute 20 second light delay. Never open
   with background.
4. One familiar object per big number. Penguins versus submarines, a plastic bottle
   versus 92 bars of pressure, an airplane's altitude versus Challenger Deep. A number
   without an object is forgotten in ten seconds.
5. Participation before explanation. Play the sound, ask the guess, then reveal. Our
   twist ritual should include a guess moment: the viewer commits to an answer before
   the machine prints the ticket.
6. The ranking, the payoff, the next question. End every video with three things:
   the answer ticket, the "what does this mean for you" line (AsapSCIENCE), and the
   next episode's question (Curiosity Hub trailer). That is the subscription loop.
7. Silence before the twist. BRAND already requires 0.4-0.8 seconds of silence before
   the twist. Every channel above uses a pause, a music drop, or a held frame at the
   turn. Do not let music run through the twist.
8. Sound is brand. Zack D's signature bed, Kurzgesagt's composed score, AsapSCIENCE's
   host-composed music. Our brand sound (lever clack, whirr, 3 chime notes) plus one
   consistent music palette across moods is what makes episode 20 recognisable as
   episode 1's sibling. That is why the music library in this engine phase matters.

## 4. What we must NOT copy

- No channel's look: not Kurzgesagt's birds, ducks, or space palette; not What If's
  3D CGI; not Zack D's 3D medical style; not Infographics' simple characters; not
  AsapSCIENCE's whiteboard; not TED-Ed's rotating freelance styles; not RealLifeLore's
  maps. Our look is code-drawn flat colour in Midnight, Lever Yellow, Twist Coral,
  Sim Teal, Paper, Steel, per BRAND section 4.
- No channel's music or narrator. No reused signature beds, no imitation of Steve
  Taylor, Peter Schmiedchen, or the Zack D narration cadence. Our Operator voice is
  one fixed voice from the approved set, and our music comes from the cleared library.
- No title formula copied word for word. "What If [thing]?" is a question shape, not
  a property, but do not lift a competitor's exact title, thumbnail composition, or
  parenthetical gimmick for the same topic.
- No disaster-only or gross-out diet. What If, Infographics, and Zack D all lean on
  death, war, and body horror for clicks. Our BRAND bans apocalypse language and
  tragedy jokes. Wonder and curiosity are the fuel, not dread.
- No fake evidence. Never use AI images of real people, documents, logos, or fake
  headlines as if they were archive. The small channels' honesty line ("scenes are
  imagined, research is real") is the floor, not the ceiling: our guesses are labelled
  on screen, in the BRAND's words, "Here's where the machine has to guess."
- No volume-at-all-costs. Infographics ships about 9 videos a week with a team;
  Zack D ships about 40 projects a week through a studio. We ship 3 long videos a week
  with a code engine. Chasing their volume with our resources would break quality and
  trip YouTube's inauthentic content rule (see _PLATFORMS.md).

## 5. Our gap (where nobody strong is standing)

- The machine format itself: lever, sim clock, answer ticket, run numbers. Nobody
  else runs every episode through the same fictional machine with the same rituals.
  Rituals are cheaper than production value and harder to copy than a look.
- Everyday questions, not just cosmic ones. The big channels fight over planets,
  asteroids, and wars. "Why do we say cheese in photos?", "What if your phone battery
  never died?", "What if money disappeared?" are uncontested, cheap to draw, and
  personal, which is why they convert to comments and guesses.
- The 5-6 minute middle. xkcd's What If? averages 3:39, Half as Interesting about
  7 minutes, Kurzgesagt about 12:20, What If about 20:23. Our 5-6 minutes sits between
  the joke and the lecture, long enough for a real answer, short enough to finish on
  a phone.
- Cadence against slowness. Kurzgesagt ships 1-2 a month, What If's momentum is
  slowing, AsapSCIENCE is roughly monthly. Three real answers a week, on schedule,
  is itself a differentiator for a new channel.
- Honesty as a feature. Date-stamped sources, guesses labelled on screen, imagined
  scenes separated from research. In a feed full of confident AI slop, a channel that
  says where it guessed is the one a viewer trusts with the next question.
