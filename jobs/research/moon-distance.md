## The question
The Moon is about 384,400 km away on average — light covers it in about 1.25 s, so a round trip takes about 2.5 s. How did people
who could never go there measure it — and how do we know it to the millimetre today?

## Facts (S = source ids below)
| # | fact | sources |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Mean Earth–Moon distance ≈ 384,400 km (≈ 60.3 Earth radii) | S8, S9 |
| F2 | Aristarchus of Samos (3rd c. BC), *On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon*, used the Earth's shadow during a lunar eclipse and the half-moon angle; his reconstructed Earth–Moon distance ≈ 20 Earth radii (too small) | S1, S2 |
| F3 | Hipparchus (2nd c. BC) refined the eclipse method: mean distance 67⅓ Earth radii (range 62–72⅔); Ptolemy later gave 59 | S1, S3 |
| F4 | 1751–52: Lacaille observed the Moon from the Cape of Good Hope while Lalande observed it at the same times from Berlin (with a borrowed quadrant): two places almost on one meridian, far apart → the Moon's parallax | S4, S5, S6 |
| F5 | Lalande's result: lunar parallax ≈ 57′ 3.7″ → about 60 Earth radii (≈ 238,700 miles in Proctor's account; old figure, said as "about") | S5, S6 |
| F6 | 10 Jan 1946, 11:58 am, Camp Evans, Belmar, New Jersey: Project Diana (US Army Signal Corps, Col. John H. DeWitt) bounced radar off the Moon; the echo came back after about 2.5 s | S7, S10, S11 |
| F7 | Apollo 11 (July 1969) left a laser retroreflector array on the Moon; on 1 Aug 1969 the 120-inch telescope at Lick Observatory fired a ruby laser at it and timed the return | S12, S13 |
| F8 | Reflectors from Apollo 11, 14, 15 and two Soviet Lunokhods are still in use; stations like APOLLO (Apache Point, New Mexico) measure the distance to about 1 mm | S13, S14, S15 |
| F9 | Very little light comes back: older stations averaged ~0.01 photons per pulse, APOLLO about 0.25 (peaks ~0.6, later ~1) | S14, S15 |
| F10 | The Moon is receding from Earth by about 3.8 cm per year (tidal friction; also slows Earth's rotation) | S8, S16 |

## Clues (story order)
1. **The shadow** — an eclipse puts the Earth's shadow on the Moon; Aristarchus/Hipparchus turn its size into a distance (F2, F3).
2. **Two places at once** — Berlin and the Cape measure the same Moon at the same moment; the tiny difference is the parallax (F4, F5).
3. **An echo** — 1946, a radar pulse goes to the Moon and comes back 2.5 s later (F6).
4. **A mirror** — Apollo 11's reflector; a laser pulse, a handful of photons home, a millimetre (F7–F9).
Twist: the Moon is leaving us, 3.8 cm a year (F10).
