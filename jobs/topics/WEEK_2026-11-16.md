### 7. The concrete that heals itself
- Working title (EN): The Concrete That Heals Itself
- Tên tiếng Việt: Bê tông La Mã tự chữa lành
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 3 (no single person or physical action at the centre; the "character" is the concrete itself, with the MIT team as the second half); 4 (stakes are abstract: buildings outlasting us).
- Hook: "Crack a Roman wall and rainwater glues it shut again."
- The turn: the white lumps everyone called sloppy mixing are the medicine.
- Facts: Roman concrete (opus caementicium) lasts about 2,000 years while modern concrete cracks in decades. For years the small white lumps in it, lime clasts, were dismissed as bad mixing. In Jan 2023 a team led by Admir Masic at MIT published in Science Advances (Seymour et al.): samples from the city wall of Privernum, Italy, show the Romans used hot mixing, adding dry quicklime straight into volcanic ash (pozzolana) and aggregate, then water. The reaction tops 200 C, leaves brittle lime clasts behind. When a crack runs through a clast and water gets in, calcium dissolves and recrystallises as calcium carbonate, sealing the crack. Lab samples with clasts sealed cracks up to 0.6 mm wide within about two weeks; samples without quicklime never sealed. A 2025 Nature Communications study of an unfinished building site in Pompeii (Domus IX 10,1) found dry-mixed quicklime, supporting hot mixing. Honest note: some scholars (Yegul and Lancaster) argue the hot-mixing evidence is slim and design matters more; the pack must present hot mixing as the leading explanation, not the only factor.
- Sources:

  2. ScienceAlert, 29 Oct 2025, "We Finally Know Why Ancient Roman Concrete Stood The Test of Time", https://www.sciencealert.com/we-finally-know-why-ancient-roman-concrete-lasts-thousands-of-years
  - Paper: Seymour et al., "Hot mixing: Mechanistic insights into the durability of ancient Roman concrete", Science Advances, Jan 2023, doi 10.1126/sciadv.add1602.

### 8. The city standing on a forest
- Working title (EN): The City Standing on a Forest
- Tên tiếng Việt: Venice đứng trên rừng gỗ
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 3 (no single person at the centre); 4 (stakes are slow: the city sinking, not one moment).
- Hook: "A city of marble palaces stands on millions of wooden sticks in the mud."
- The turn: the wood never turned to stone, and most piles do not even reach the hard clay.
- Facts: Venice stands on millions of wooden piles (alder, larch, oak, elm) driven into the lagoon mud. The piles are short, 1 to 3.5 m long, 100-250 mm across, set close together. They survive because they sit permanently under water in oxygen-poor mud, where the fungi and bacteria that rot wood cannot work. Popular retellings say the wood "petrified into stone"; that is overstated, the timber is still wood, mineral-infiltrated at the surface. Before the 20th century the piles were too short to reach the hard clay layer (caranto); they worked by packing and densifying the mud, not by reaching bedrock. On top: timber planks, then Istrian stone, then brick. Rialto Bridge: about 12,000 piles, about 6,000 under each abutment. The real threat today is not rot but rising seas, boat wash at the waterline, and 20th-century groundwater pumping, which caused subsidence.

  2. Engineering Rome, "Venice: the Design and the Future of the City", https://engineeringrome.org/tabatha/ (cites Bernabei et al., Journal of Cultural Heritage, 2019, doi 10.1016/j.culher.2018.07.015)

### 9. The dancing plague of 1518
- Working title (EN): The Dancing Plague of 1518
- Tên tiếng Việt: Cơn dịch nhảy múa năm 1518
- Pillar: strange-but-true events
- Score: 9/10. Weak: 1 (death toll disputed; say "a number died", never "hundreds died"); 8 (well known among history fans).
- Hook: "One woman started dancing in the street and could not stop."
- The turn: the city hired more musicians to cure it, and the dancing spread.
- Facts: Strasbourg, July 1518. A woman recorded as Frau Troffea stepped into the street on 14 July and began dancing, for four to six days. Within a month the Imlin family chronicle says 400 citizens were seized. The mania lasted from mid-July to late August or early September. The city physicians said the dancers had "hot blood" and the cure was to dance it out, so the council hired musicians and built stages. People died of exhaustion, stroke and heart failure; the toll is unknown and disputed, the "15 deaths a day at the peak" figure is a later claim, not in the council minutes. Then the council banned public dancing and sent the afflicted to pray at the shrine of St Vitus in Saverne. Modern explanations: ergot poisoning (debunked by historian John Waller: ergot restricts blood flow, so victims could not dance for days) and mass psychogenic illness under famine and disease stress, inside a culture that feared the curse of St Vitus. Waller's book: A Time to Dance, a Time to Die (2008).

### 10. The explosion with no crater
- Working title (EN): The Explosion with No Crater
- Tên tiếng Việt: Vụ nổ trên trời Siberia
- Pillar: strange-but-true events

- Facts: 30 June 1908, about 7:15 a.m., over the Podkamennaya Tunguska River, central Siberia. A fireball crossed the sky and exploded about 6-10 km up. Almost no one died: NASA's 2008 account found no direct evidence of deaths; later research suggests very few. 30 June is now International Asteroid Day (UN, 2016).

In 1934 farmers claimed over 57,000 bounties in six months, doing the job themselves.

### 13. Why ice cream hurts your head
- Working title (EN): Why Ice Cream Hurts Your Head
- Tên tiếng Việt: Vì sao ăn kem bị buốt đầu
- Pillar: your body in one concrete situation
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 4 (stakes are low: a headache); 7 (emotion is curiosity, mild).
- Hook: "You bite your ice cream and your brain floods itself with blood."
- The turn: the pain is your own blood, rushing in to keep your brain warm.
- Facts: Brain freeze, medical name sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia. In 2012 Jorge Serrador of Harvard Medical School and colleagues (VA New Jersey, National University of Ireland Galway) ran the test at Experimental Biology 2012, published in The FASEB Journal: 13 healthy volunteers sipped ice water through a straw pressed against the upper palate while transcranial Doppler tracked blood flow. The anterior cerebral artery, which feeds the front of the brain, dilated rapidly and flooded the brain with blood exactly when the pain hit; when the artery constricted again, the pain vanished. The team's theory: the brain is temperature-sensitive and must keep working, so it floods itself with warm blood as self-defence; the skull is a closed box, so the extra blood briefly raises pressure, and that pressure is the pain. The pain is felt in the forehead, not the mouth, because the trigeminal nerve carries the signal and the brain misreads where it came from (referred pain). The brain itself has no pain receptors. A 2002 BMJ study (145 students) found eating ice cream in under 5 seconds caused more headaches than eating it over 30 seconds. Cure on screen: press a warm tongue to the palate or drink something warm.

  2. ScienceDaily, 22 Apr 2012, "Changes in brain's blood flow could cause 'brain freeze'", http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120422231742.htm

### 14. Why your fingers wrinkle in water
- Working title (EN): Why Your Fingers Wrinkle in Water
- Tên tiếng Việt: Vì sao ngón tay nhăn khi ngâm nước
- Pillar: your body in one concrete situation
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 4 (no stakes); 1 (the grip benefit is disputed: present it as unsettled, never as fact).
- Hook: "Soak your fingers and your nerves deflate them from inside."
- The turn: it is not the water soaking in, and scientists still argue whether the wrinkles help you grip.
- Facts: Wrinkling is not osmosis and not the skin swelling. It is an active reflex run by the sympathetic nervous system: nerves constrict tiny blood vessels (glomus bodies) under the fingertip, the pulp loses volume, the skin buckles into ridges. Known since 1935: people with nerve damage in the hand do not wrinkle. It takes 5-30 minutes, fastest in warm water around 40 C. In 2013 Kareklas, Nettle and Smulders (Newcastle University, Biology Letters) tested 20 volunteers moving wet marbles and weights: wrinkled fingers were about 12% faster with wet objects, no difference with dry objects. In 2014 Haseleu and colleagues (Max Delbruck Center, Berlin, PLoS ONE, 40 participants) found no advantage. A 2021 study (Nick Davis, Manchester Metropolitan) found wrinkled fingers needed less pinch force on wet objects. Honest line for the script: why it happens is settled (nerves); how much it helps grip is still being measured.
- Sources:
  1. PubMed, Kareklas, Nettle, Smulders, Biology Letters 2013, 9:20120999, "Water-induced finger wrinkles improve handling of wet objects", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23302867/
  2. West Texas A and M University, Christopher S. Baird, 18 Feb 2015, "Why do my fingers absorb water and become wrinkled?", https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2015/02/18/why-do-my-fingers-absorb-water-and-become-wrinkled/
  - Dispute source for the pack: Haseleu et al., "Water-Induced Finger Wrinkles Do Not Affect Touch Acuity or Dexterity in Handling Wet Objects", PLoS ONE 9(1), 2014, e84949.

### 15. The man who hiccuped for 68 years
- Working title (EN): The Man Who Hiccuped for 68 Years
- Tên tiếng Việt: Người nấc 68 năm không ngừng
- Pillar: your body in one concrete situation
- Score: 9/10. Weak: 4 (stakes are low: 68 years of annoyance, not danger).
- Hook: "He fell off a pig and hiccuped for the next sixty-eight years."
- The turn: one morning in 1990, for no reason anyone knows, the hiccups just stopped.
- Facts: Charles Osborne, Iowa farmer, born 1893. On 13 June 1922, near Union, Nebraska, he was hanging a 350-pound hog for butchering, lifted it, fell, and started hiccuping. He hiccuped 20-40 times a minute, an estimated 430 million times, for 68 years, even in his sleep. He learned a special way of breathing to hide the sound. Dr Terence Anthoney, who examined him in the 1980s, believed the fall damaged a small part of the brain stem that suppresses the hiccup reflex; neurosurgeon Ali Seifi later suggested a rib injury to the diaphragm or a small stroke. No cure ever worked for long. He appeared on The Tonight Show and That's Incredible! (1980). In 1990 the hiccups stopped, for no known reason (Guinness says one morning in February 1990; other accounts say June 1990; use "in 1990" only). He died in May 1991, aged 97, of ulcer complications. Guinness World Record: longest attack of hiccups, still standing.
