### 5. The 63-year-old who rode Niagara Falls in a barrel
- Working title (EN): The 63-Year-Old Who Rode Niagara Falls in a Barrel
- Tên tiếng Việt: Bà giáo 63 tuổi vượt thác Niagara trong thùng gỗ
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 9/10. Weak: 8 (well known in the US).
- Hook: "She was 63, broke, and widowed, so she climbed into a pickle barrel and went over Niagara Falls."
- The turn: she tested the barrel on her cat first, and the stunt that was supposed to make her rich left her broke anyway.
- Human thread: her cat. Two days before the plunge she sent the cat over the falls in the same barrel; the cat survived with a cut on its head, and posed with her in photographs afterwards.
- Facts: Annie Edson Taylor, born 24 Oct 1838, schoolteacher, widowed, her only child died in infancy. By 1901 she was broke in Bay City, Michigan, and planned a stunt for money: ride over Niagara Falls in a barrel. The barrel was custom-made, white oak slats with iron rings, about 5 feet tall and 3 feet wide, padded with a mattress, with a bicycle pump to seal it. She advertised her age as 43. On 24 Oct 1901, her 63rd birthday, at about 4 p.m., she climbed in with her heart-shaped pillow, was towed out and went over the Canadian Horseshoe Falls, a 51 m (167 ft) drop. Rescuers pulled the barrel out about 20 minutes later; she emerged with only a small gash on her head, saying: "I prayed every second I was in the barrel except for a few seconds after the fall when I went unconscious." She became the "Queen of the Mist", the first person to survive a trip over Niagara Falls in a barrel. The money never came; she died 29 Apr 1921, aged 82, as broke as she had feared. Her gravestone reads: FIRST TO GO OVER THE HORSESHOE FALL IN A BARREL AND LIVE. Since her, 15 people have tried it; 5 died. It is illegal now.

### 6. The doctor who took the handle off a pump
- Working title (EN): The Doctor Who Took the Handle Off a Pump
- Tên tiếng Việt: Bác sĩ tháo tay bơm nước và dừng dịch tả
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 4 (stakes are an epidemic, slow, not one moment); 8 (very well known).
- Hook: "Six hundred people died in ten days, and the killer was the water pump on their own street."
- The turn: he drew a dot map, and the dots made a circle around one pump; the handle came off, and the dying stopped.
- Human thread: the widow in Hampstead who had Broad Street water carted to her house because she liked the taste, and died of cholera for it; and the brewery workers next door who drank beer instead of pump water and did not fall ill.
- Facts: John Snow, London physician, 1854. On 31 Aug 1854 a cholera outbreak hit Soho, London; about 600 deaths in ten days (616 in the parish records; 578 in the reappraisal). The whole city believed disease spread through bad air ("miasma"). Snow interviewed families house by house with Reverend Henry Whitehead and plotted every death on a map: the deaths clustered around the public water pump on Broad Street (now Broadwick Street). People who drank from it died; the brewery workers who drank beer and the people who used other pumps did not. On 7 Sep 1854 he put the map before the Board of Guardians of St James parish; the next day they removed the pump handle. The epidemic was already declining as people fled, but it disappeared within days. Later the well was found dug less than a metre from an old cesspit leaking sewage into the water. Snow's map founded modern epidemiology.

### 7. The tunnel under the river, dug by a shipworm
- Working title (EN): The Tunnel Under the River, Dug by a Shipworm
- Tên tiếng Việt: Đường hầm dưới sông Thames, học từ con hà biển
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 9/10. Weak: 8 (well known among engineering fans).
- Hook: "A worm that eats wooden ships taught two men how to dig under a river."
- The turn: the river broke in four times, nearly killed the son, and he went back down in a diving bell to plug the hole with bags of clay.
- Human thread: father and son. Marc Brunel, 56, invented the shield; his 21-year-old son Isambard ran the dig, threw his birthday party inside the tunnel, and nearly drowned in it.
- Facts: Marc Isambard Brunel, French-born engineer, watched the shipworm Teredo navalis bore into wooden hulls and line the hole as it went. In Jan 1818 he and Lord Cochrane patented a tunnelling shield: an iron frame about 11 m wide and 6.5 m high, divided into 36 one-man cells; each miner dug a few inches, the shield was screwed forward, and bricklayers lined the tunnel behind it. Digging began at Rotherhithe in March 1825. The tunnel flooded on 17-18 May 1827; Isambard, 21, went down in a diving bell and plugged the hole in the riverbed with bags of clay. It flooded again 12 Jan 1828, killing six men and nearly killing Isambard; work stopped for seven years for lack of money. Completed Nov 1841, opened to the public 25 Mar 1843: 396 m long, 23 m under the river, the first tunnel ever built under a navigable river. One million people visited in the first 15 weeks. Cost 634,000 pounds (about 67 million in 2023 money). Every soft-ground tunnel since uses Brunel's shield.

### 8. The tower that was supposed to be torn down
- Working title (EN): The Tower That Was Supposed to Be Torn Down
- Tên tiếng Việt: Tháp Eiffel suýt bị tháo dỡ, radio đã cứu nó
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 3 (no single person at the centre: Eiffel and Captain Ferrie share it); 4 (stakes are abstract: a tower's survival); 8 (very famous).
- Hook: "Paris built the most famous tower on Earth, then planned to tear it down after twenty years."
- The turn: the man who built it turned his own tower into a radio mast, and the army could not afford to lose it.
- Human thread: Gustave Eiffel himself. He kept a small apartment at the top, received Thomas Edison there, built a wind tunnel at the base and ran about 5,000 aerodynamic tests between 1909 and 1911, all to give the tower a reason to live.
- Facts: The Eiffel Tower was built for the 1889 Paris Exposition Universelle: 18,038 iron parts, 2.5 million rivets, built in two years, two months and five days, against a protest signed by famous French artists. Eiffel got a 20-year concession; the tower was due to be dismantled in 1909 and the land returned to the City of Paris. Eiffel had planned for this from the start: he gave the tower scientific uses (weather station from 1889, physics experiments). The decisive one was wireless telegraphy: on 5 Nov 1898 Eugene Ducretet sent the first radio contact in Morse from the tower to the Pantheon, 4 km away. In 1903 Eiffel offered the tower to Captain Gustave Ferrie of the army and paid for an antenna stand at the summit himself. By 1908 the tower transmitted and received at 6,000 km, the longest range in the world. In 1909 a permanent military station was built under the Champ de Mars; the city renewed Eiffel's concession for another 70 years from 1 Jan 1910. From 1910 it transmitted international time signals. Today it is 330 m tall and had 6.75 million visitors in 2025.

### 9. The man who survived two atomic bombs
- Working title (EN): The Man Who Survived Two Atomic Bombs
- Tên tiếng Việt: Người đàn ông sống sót sau cả hai quả bom nguyên tử

- The turn: the second bomb fell at 11:02 a.m., while he was in the office describing the first one.
- Human thread: his wife Hisako and his infant son Katsutoshi, waiting for him in Nagasaki. He was on his last day in Hiroshima and rushing home to them; his survival story, told to his family, is also what kept them from the worst of the second blast.
- Facts: Tsutomu Yamaguchi, 29, naval design engineer for Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, on a three-month assignment in Hiroshima. 6 Aug 1945 was his last day; at 8:15 a.m. he was walking to the docks about 3 km from the hypocentre when Little Boy exploded. Burns to the left side of his body, both eardrums ruptured, temporary blindness, hair burned off. He spent the night in a shelter, then took a train home to Nagasaki the next day. On 9 Aug, at 11 a.m., he was in the Mitsubishi office in Nagasaki describing the Hiroshima blast to his supervisor; at 11:02 Fat Man detonated about 3 km away, blowing out the office windows and knocking him down. About 165 people are thought to have experienced both bombings; Yamaguchi was the only one officially recognised by the Japanese government as a nijuu hibakusha (twice-bombed person), certified 24 Mar 2009. He lived to 93, dying 4 Jan 2010 of stomach cancer. At 90 he spoke at the United Nations in New York: "Having experienced atomic bombing twice and survived, it is my destiny that I must speak out."

- Facts: Michel Lotito, born 16 Jun 1950 in Grenoble, France, known as Monsieur Mangetout ("Mr Eat-All"). Between 1959 and 1997 he reportedly ate about nine tons of metal, including 18 bicycles, 15 shopping carts, 7 television sets, 6 chandeliers, 2 beds, a pair of skis, a computer and a coffin. His most famous feat: a Cessna 150 light aircraft, about 1,100 kg, eaten piece by piece between 1978 and 1980. Guinness gave him the record for the strangest diet, then retired the category. He died in 2007, aged 57, of natural causes.

- Human thread: your own skin. The reflex is older than us: every hair on your body except your palms and soles has this muscle, wired to the same fight-or-flight system as a frightened cat.
- Facts: Goosebumps, piloerection: a tiny smooth muscle at the base of each hair follicle, the arrector pili, contracts and pulls the hair upright, puckering the skin into a bump. Music-triggered goosebumps, called frisson or aesthetic chills, track the brain's reward system. A 2020 Harvard study found the sympathetic nerves around the arrector pili also wrap around hair follicle stem cells, and during prolonged cold they help activate those stem cells to grow new hair.
