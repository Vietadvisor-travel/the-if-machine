### 4. The man who walked 160 kilometres alone across Antarctica
- Working title (EN): The Man Who Walked 160 Kilometres Alone Across Antarctica

- Working title (EN): The Postman Who Built a Palace from Stones
- Tên tiếng Việt: Người đưa thư xây cung điện bằng đá trong 33 năm

- Facts: Indianapolis, November 1930. The 7-story Indiana Bell Building, an 11,000-ton (22-million-pound) telephone exchange, stood in the way of a bigger headquarters. The 600 employees kept working inside the whole time; gas, water, electricity, sewage and the telephone service never stopped, carried on flexible lines, and the entrance moved with the building on a curved sidewalk. Workers said they did not feel it move. The building served until the late 1950s and was demolished in 1963; the site now holds AT&T offices.

### 8. The woman who mapped the ocean floor
- Working title (EN): The Woman Who Mapped the Ocean Floor
- Tên tiếng Việt: Người phụ nữ vẽ bản đồ đáy biển mà cả thế giới không tin
- Pillar: history under your feet
- Score: 9/10. Weak: 3 (Tharp and Heezen share the story; Tharp is the centre); 6 (the discovery is a pattern on paper; the rift valley is drawn as a 2D map layer).
- Hook: "She found a valley at the bottom of the ocean that proved the continents move. Her boss called it girl talk."
- The turn: she redrew the same profiles twice more, matched them against earthquake epicentres, and the pattern was impossible to ignore.
- Human thread: the drafting table. She was never allowed on a research ship, so she mapped the whole ocean floor from a desk, with pens, ink and rulers.
- Facts: Marie Tharp (1920-2006), geologist at Columbia University's Lamont Geological Observatory from 1948. Women were not allowed on research vessels, so while Bruce Heezen collected sonar data at sea, Tharp plotted depth profiles at her desk. In 1952, working across six parallel profiles of the North Atlantic, she found a continuous V-shaped rift valley running down the centre of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. She showed it to Heezen; he groaned, said "It cannot be. It looks too much like continental drift," and dismissed her reading as "girl talk." She redrew the profiles and showed the rift matched newly mapped earthquake epicentres; it took about a year to convince him. In 1956 they announced the find; in 1957 they published the first detailed physiographic diagram of the ocean floor. The mid-ocean ridge system runs more than 40,000 miles through the world's oceans. In 1997 the Library of Congress named Tharp one of the four greatest cartographers of the 20th century.

### 9. The man who sold the Eiffel Tower twice
- Working title (EN): The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower Twice
- Tên tiếng Việt: Kẻ lừa đảo bán tháp Eiffel hai lần
- Pillar: strange-but-true events

- Hook: "He sold the Eiffel Tower for scrap. Then he came back and tried to sell it again."
- The turn: the buyer never reported the crime, because reporting it meant admitting he had bribed a government official.
- Human thread: Andre Poisson, the mark. A newcomer to Paris, eager to make his name, who paid for a tower he could never own and was too ashamed to tell anyone.
- Facts: Victor Lustig (1890-1947), a Bohemian-born con man. In spring 1925 he read a newspaper piece about the Eiffel Tower's maintenance costs, posed as deputy director-general of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, forged official letterheads, and invited six Paris scrap dealers to a confidential lunch at the Hotel de Crillon. He toured them round the tower in a government car as if inspecting his own property. Andre Poisson, an ambitious newcomer to the scrap trade, won the bid and paid about 70,000 francs, payment plus a bribe to the "official". Poisson realised he had been conned and did not go to the police. Lustig fled to Austria, then returned to Paris months later and tried the same scam again; this time a buyer grew suspicious before the deal closed and Lustig fled France. He later ran a counterfeiting ring in the United States, was arrested in New York on 10 May 1935, got 20 years, and died 11 Mar 1947 of pneumonia in a prison hospital.

### 10. The thorn hedge that taxed a continent's salt
- Working title (EN): The Thorn Hedge That Taxed a Continent's Salt
- Tên tiếng Việt: Bức tường gai 4.000 cây số nước Anh trồng để đánh thuế muối
- Pillar: strange-but-true events
- Score: 8/10. Weak: 3 (no single person at the centre; Roy Moxham, the rediscoverer, is the anchor); 4 (stakes are a tax, abstract); 1 (figures vary: the customs LINE ran 2,504 miles, the living hedge itself was shorter; say the line ran 2,504 miles and the living hedge was part of it).
- Hook: "The British grew a wall of thorns four thousand kilometres long across India, to tax the salt in your food."
- The turn: the wall was forgotten so completely that a librarian in London had to prove it had ever existed, and he found one low embankment in a field.
- Human thread: Roy Moxham, a book conservator who found the hedge in a footnote in 1995 and spent three trips across India looking for it.
- Facts: The Inland Customs Line, British India. Customs houses were set up in Bengal from 1803 to stop salt smuggling, because salt was heavily taxed. The line grew until, by 1869, it ran 4,030 km (2,504 miles) from the Himalayan foothills to Orissa near the Bay of Bengal. From the 1840s it was a dry hedge of thorny brushwood (Indian plum, babool, karonda, prickly pear); from the 1860s, under Commissioner Allan Octavian Hume, it became a living hedge, up to 12 ft high, guarded by about 12,000 men. In 1869-70 the line collected about 12.5 million rupees in salt tax. It was abolished 1 Apr 1879, when the British took over the Sambhar Salt Lake and taxed salt at the point of manufacture; the salt tax itself lasted until 1946, and Gandhi's Salt March of 1930 protested it. The hedge was forgotten until Roy Moxham, a conservator at a University of London library, found a footnote about it in 1995, made three trips to India, found a low embankment in Etawah district in 1998, and published "The Great Hedge of India" in 2001.

### 15. Why your voice sounds strange on a recording
- Working title (EN): Why Your Voice Sounds Strange on a Recording
- Tên tiếng Việt: Vì sao giọng bạn trong bản ghi âm nghe lạ thế
- Pillar: your body in one concrete situation

- The turn: you have never actually heard your real voice. Everyone else has.
- Human thread: you. The voice you have listened to all your life is a mix nobody else ever hears.
- Facts: When you speak, your voice reaches your own ears by two routes at once. Air conduction: sound leaves your mouth, travels through the air into your ear canal and vibrates your eardrum; this is the only way anyone else hears you, and the only way a microphone hears you. Bone conduction: the vibration of your vocal cords travels through the bones and soft tissue of your jaw and skull straight to the cochlea, skipping the outer ear. Bone is a low-pass filter: low frequencies travel through it better than high ones, so the voice inside your head is deeper and fuller than the one other people hear. A recording has only the air half, so it sounds thinner and higher to you. Measured: Porschmann 2000 (Acta Acustica) found bone conduction adds a significant boost at 700-1,200 Hz; Stenfelt and Reinfeldt 2007 (Journal of the Acoustical Society of America) confirmed it with accelerometers on the skull; Tchumatchenko and Reichenbach 2014 (Nature Communications) showed bone conduction favours low frequencies. The dislike has a name since 1966: voice confrontation. And the cringe is one-sided: Hughes and Harrison 2013 found people rated their own recorded voice more attractive than strangers rated it.
